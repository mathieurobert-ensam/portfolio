Restoring the Campus Clock

Designing the energy system of a pendulum clock for a historic building, then making its parts in the school workshop.

ContextYear-long project, Arts et Métiers Lille
Team5 students · mentoring by Oresys
My roleDesigner of the winding system; project organization
Year2025–2026
Objective

The clock above the main courtyard of the Lille campus, a listed building, lost its mechanism decades ago. A first student team modeled a pendulum movement. Our goal: give it an automatic winding system, manufacture its brass parts in the school workshop and prepare the assembly, without modifying the building.

STEP 01

The starting point

The hands of the clock have been stopped for decades. The previous team left a 3D model of the movement, but no energy system, and drawings that the workshop could not use.

The key challenge: the clock is driven by a falling weight. When the weight reaches the bottom it must be wound back up, and with a simple motor the torque on the escapement would be interrupted: the clock would stop and have to be reset.

STEP 02

Designing the winding system (my main contribution)

I proposed a differential (epicyclic gear train): the motor drives the planet carrier, one output drives the movement and the other the drum carrying the weight. The differential did not exist even as a sketch, so I designed the whole energy block in CAD: housing, planet gears, drum, shafts and freewheel.

ω41 + ω42 = 2·ω2  ·  C41 = C42 = C2/2
  • The movement turns very slowly, so the motor torque is shared equally between lifting the weight and driving the clock: the torque on the escapement is never interrupted.
  • Limit switches start and stop the motor at the bottom and top of the weight's travel.
  • The motor can be disengaged to wind the clock by hand with a handwheel if it fails, as required by the specification.

STEP 03

Making the parts manufacturable

Moving from CAD to the workshop revealed many problems:

  • The drum could not be machined with the tools available for the 5-axis machine, so I split it into several parts assembled with screws.
  • The drawings: about fifty had to be redone. With our professor, we drew them first without dimensions, agreed on the useful information, and corrected them only once, with fits such as H7/g6/m6.
  • Inherited parts that could not be assembled were redesigned: a pendulum taking a bolt, a missing groove on the escape wheel, brazed joints, plain bearings.
  • A 3D-printed scale model of the whole mechanism (about 15 × 15 cm) revealed problems in the coupling between the winding system and the escapement, before machining the rest of the parts.

STEP 04

Manufacturing in the school workshop

  • Gear blanks programmed in TopSolid and machined on a DMU 50 5-axis center, held by a dedicated fixture designed by the team to keep them concentric.
  • Gear cutting: we brought back into service a gear hobbing machine unused for more than ten years. Tooth counts such as 19, 38, 57 or 200 cannot be divided directly, so the change gears are found by differential indexing:
(76 / 30) · (A / B) · (C / D) = Z
  • Shafts turned in brass on a manual lathe following process sheets (six phases for a 97 mm shaft with a 30-tooth pinion).

STEP 05

Organizing the project and handing it over

We were falling behind, and the files inherited from the previous team were hard to use. I asked my mentor at the consulting firm Oresys for help, and their team received us in their Lille office. With their advice we set up:

  • a shared Gantt chart, re-planned on the real progress;
  • a bill of materials of about fifty parts in five functional groups;
  • a naming system for the CAD files (for example PI-TR-Shaft 3);
  • a complete handover package for the next team: BOM, CAD, drawings, CAM programs, machined and raw parts, and the status of each sub-assembly.
Results
  • Winding system fully designed: differential, drum, freewheel and manual back-up winding.
  • Gear train validated: 60:1 then 12:1, a total ratio of 720 between the escapement and the hour hand.
  • Gear hobbing machine back in service; three parts machined (two shafts and a pinion) and gear blanks ready to be cut.
  • Project handed over to the next team and to HorloZ'Arts, the campus watchmaking association.

What I learned: a part only exists once it can be made. Functional dimensioning and an early dialogue with the workshop matter as much as the design itself.
