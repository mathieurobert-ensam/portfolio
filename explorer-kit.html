From a Designer's Concept to an Aluminium Prototype

Bringing the engineering view to a product imagined by a design student, and building it with the means of the school workshop.

ContextProcess & methods project, Arts et Métiers × ESAAT design school
Team2 students
My roleProject lead; workshop operations
YearSpring 2026
Objective

A design student imagined a kit that helps children explore nature: an open aluminium case, interchangeable plates held by elastic bands, and a universal hook for a bike, a branch or a backpack. Our job: turn the concept into a technical specification, size the parts without betraying the design intent, and build a prototype in the school workshop within a €1,200 budget.

STEP 01

From design intent to a specification

The design intentions had to become measurable requirements, compatible with the materials and machines of the workshop.

  • Hold interchangeable plates with an elastic band, and attach the case to varied supports.
  • Be handled by a five-year-old: low effort, no sharp edges.
  • Stay light and stiff enough, look like anodized aluminium, and respect the budget.

With my teammate, we wrote the specification and held weekly workshop sessions documented by systematic minutes (done, to do, problems).

3D-printed mock-up of the open case on the aluminium sheet
STEP 02

Choosing the material and the architecture

  • Steel or aluminium: compared for weight, stiffness and cost, with deflection simulations for several sheet thicknesses. Aluminium gave the best compromise.
  • Open or closed case: a closed case needed two parts and an assembly step. Clinching was not available, seaming adds three sheet thicknesses at the joint, and welding is poorly suited to thin aluminium. We chose an open case, which removes the assembly altogether.

STEP 03

Designing for the process

  • Before forming the metal, we calculated the flat blanks from the neutral fiber, taking the bend allowance into account, so that the finished parts would be the right size.
  • The length of the open edge was adapted so that the press brake could bend the whole case in a single pass.
  • The press-brake punch turned out smaller than planned, so some dimensions were adapted during manufacturing: direct feedback from methods to design.

The band saw proved too inaccurate on thin sheet, hence the guillotine shear. Every edge was deburred, essential for a product used by young children.

Prototype parts
STEP 05

Forming the motifs: comparing processes

To reproduce organic shapes (a leaf, a shell, a stone) on the plates, several processes were tested, and both successes and failures were recorded:

  • CNC engraving of the motifs.
  • Embossing with a 3D-printed punch and die: a clean shell motif, but the tooling could not be reused, as the pressure had damaged the plastic.
  • Rubber-pad forming (Guérin process), studied as an alternative.
  • CNC machining in the composites department.

Results
  • A bent prototype of the open case in 2 mm aluminium, validating that the case can be bent in a single pass, and several formed plates.
  • As project lead, I did not freeze the specification early enough: the requirements changed at each review, and we did not reach the final version of the case or all the planned plates. With other trades, scope and deliverables must be fixed from the start.

What I learned: choose manufacturing processes that suit the product, and take process constraints into account while designing a part, not afterwards. A 3D-printed tool is enough for one prototype; series production would demand tooling built to last.
